Cards group what people need to know about one thing, like a project, a product or a person, so they can scan a page of them and pick one. They look like a box with a picture in it, which is why they're so often built with only a mouse in mind. Think of a card wrapped in a link, which a screen reader reads out from its first word to its last as the link's name, a `<div>` with a click handler, which keyboard users can't reach at all, or a Save button nested inside that link, which HTML doesn't allow.

This page walks you through building one the right way. We'll look at:
- **User Interface** (UI) - how a card looks
- **User Experience** (UX) - how it behaves for every person and every input
- **Developer Experience** (DX) - how developers work with it
- **Agentic Experience** (AX) - how agents can read and verify it

Every example on this page runs on our [reference implementation](/docs/components/card#reference-implementation), which does its part of every rule in the [checklist](/docs/components/card#checklist), so feel free to tab through them as you read. The code under each example comes in React, Vue, Svelte, Angular, Solid, Astro and Vanilla, written for a Card with the same API in each. Pick yours from the Framework select above the sidebar. Vanilla is plain HTML and JavaScript, so it writes out the markup from the [DOM contract](/docs/components/card#deterministic).

A card is a container rather than a control, so most of what it does comes from the elements in it: a heading, a link when the whole card takes you somewhere, and [Buttons](/docs/components/button) for anything else people can do with it.

## At a glance

| Aspect   | Card                                                                                                |
| -------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Element  | `<article>`, or a `<section>`, `<div>` or `<li>` with `as`                                          |
| Role     | `article`, or `listitem` as an `<li>`                                                               |
| Name     | Its heading, which also names its link when the card is one                                         |
| Keyboard | `Tab` to its link, then to its actions, and `Enter` to follow the link                              |
| States   | Hover and focus, when it's a link                                                                   |
| Parts    | Media, a header with the heading and an eyebrow, body, actions and footer, of which only the header and its heading are always there |
| Pattern  | None in the APG, since an [`<article>`](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/sections.html#the-article-element) and a link cover it |
| WCAG 2.2 | 1.1.1, 1.3.1, 1.3.2, 1.4.3, 1.4.11, 2.1.1, 2.4.4, 2.4.6, 2.4.7 and 4.1.2, among others              |

## User Interface (UI)

Let's start with how a card looks: its parts, what goes in each of them, how cards sit together on a page and how a card shows its states.

### Anatomy

::card-anatomy
1. **Container** — The root, an `<article>` by default, which draws the border and holds the parts.
2. **Media** — Optional. An image, shown at the top, edge to edge.
3. **Eyebrow** — Optional. A short line in the header, shown above the heading, like a category.
4. **Heading** — The header's heading, which says what the card is about. When the card is a link, the link is in here.
5. **Body** — Optional. A sentence or two, and any details, like a date or a price, set apart from the header by a border.
6. **Actions** — Optional. Buttons that act on the card, like Save, right after the body.
7. **Footer** — Optional. A last section, set apart by a border, for details like who made it or when.
8. **Focus ring** — An outline around the whole card when its link has focus, drawn outside it so nothing moves.
::

Every card has a container and a header with a heading in it. The header, the body, the actions and the footer are sections that run edge to edge, and a border sets the header apart from what follows it, and the footer from what comes before it. On screen, the media comes first, then the eyebrow and the heading, but in the DOM the heading comes first of all, with the eyebrow and the media after it (see [Put the heading first](/docs/components/card#put-the-heading-first)). The parts always render in the same order, which you can see in the [DOM contract](/docs/components/card#deterministic).

### Content

A card can be as small as a heading and a sentence, or hold every part. You can put any image in `media`, a short line like a category in `eyebrow`, [Buttons](/docs/components/button) in `actions` and details like the author in `footer`, while the body is whatever you put inside the card.

- Keep the heading short, and make it the thing people are choosing between, like the project's name, so they can tell cards apart by their headings alone.
- The eyebrow is for a word or two that sorts the card, like a category or a date. Anything longer belongs in the body, where it doesn't push the heading down.
- An image that only illustrates the heading, like a product photo next to the product's name, has an empty `alt`, so screen readers skip it rather than read the name twice. You'll find more under [Describe the media](/docs/components/card#describe-the-media).
- Keep the body to a sentence or two. A card is a summary of a page, and people go to that page for the rest.
- We recommend at most two actions, ranked like any other Buttons, with an `outline` or `ghost` Button rather than a `solid` one, so a page of cards doesn't fill up with primary actions.
- The footer is for details about the card rather than about its subject, like who made it, when it was updated or how many comments it has. Its text is smaller and lighter than the body's, at 4.5:1 like the eyebrow, so it doesn't compete with the body.

### Layout

A set of cards is a list, so put them in a `<ul>`, with each card as an `<li>` (`as="li"`). Screen readers then say how many cards there are, and let people skip past the whole list. A grid that fits as many cards as there's room for, each at least a set width, works on every screen without a breakpoint:

::framework-switcher

```css
.projects {
  display: grid;
  grid-template-columns: repeat(auto-fill, minmax(min(16rem, 100%), 1fr));
  gap: 1rem;
  margin: 0;
  padding: 0;
  list-style: none;
```

- The `min(16rem, 100%)` lets a card get narrower than 16rem on a narrow screen rather than overflow it, so the page never scrolls sideways at 320px wide ([WCAG 1.4.10](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow.html)).
- Cards in a row of a grid stretch to the same height, and the body takes the spare height, so the actions and the footers line up from card to card.
- Keep the cards in the same order in the DOM as on screen, and don't reorder them with `order`, `row-reverse` or `grid-auto-flow: dense`. `Tab` follows the DOM, so a card that's moved on screen makes focus jump around ([WCAG 1.3.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/meaningful-sequence.html), [2.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-order.html)).
- Safari has dropped the list role from lists with `list-style: none`, so VoiceOver doesn't say how many items there are. If that matters to you, add `role="list"` to the `<ul>`.

| State | Selector                                                   | Recommended look                                              |
| ----- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hover | `:has(a[href]:hover)` on the root, inside `@media (hover: hover)` | The borders at 45% of the text colour, up from 20%, and the heading underlined |
| Focus | `a[href]:focus-visible::after`, the link's pseudo-element  | A 2px outline, 2px outside the card, in `--color--focus`      |

Both states read the link, rather than the card. That way, the card only looks hovered while the pointer is over the part of it that follows the link, and not over one of its actions, which shows its own hover instead. None of the states change the card's size or position, and you'll find how they behave under [Every state](/docs/components/card#every-state).

### Tokens

The card reads two [theme tokens](/docs/foundations/design-tokens#theme-tokens), which every component shares:

| Token                   | Used for                                                                          |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `--color--neutral-text` | The text, and mixed into the borders, the eyebrow, the footer and the media's placeholder |
| `--color--focus`        | The focus ring, which is the same for every component                            |

Everything else is mixed from the text colour: 70% for the eyebrow and the footer, which need 4.5:1, and 20% for the borders at rest, the card's and the ones between its sections, and 45% on hover. With our reference tokens, from the Tailwind CSS palette, the eyebrow and the footer come to 5.6:1 on white and 7.4:1 on zinc-900, the same as the Input's placeholder. The borders are lighter than the Input's, since a card's heading says what it is, and WCAG only asks for 3:1 on a boundary people need to see to find the component ([WCAG 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). You'll find the tokens themselves in the Button's [`tokens.css`](/docs/components/button#reference-implementation), which you can copy into your theme.

If you need to tune a card, you can set its [own variables](/docs/foundations/design-tokens#component-variables), such as `--card--padding`, `--card--background` or `--card--media--aspect-ratio`, from a class. The Card's styles sit in the `components` cascade layer, so your class wins without any specificity tricks:

## User Experience (UX)

Now let's look at how a card behaves for every person, input and setting. A good card is [accessible](/docs/components/card#accessible), [predictable](/docs/components/card#predictable), designed in [every state](/docs/components/card#every-state) and [adaptive](/docs/components/card#adaptive).

### Accessible

#### Use the right element

The Card renders an `<article>` by default, which HTML defines as a self-contained piece of a page, one you could take out and still make sense of, like a project, a product or a post. That's what most cards are. The `as` prop picks another element when a card isn't one of those:

| Element     | `as`        | When                                                                                     |
| ----------- | ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `<article>` | The default | The card stands on its own, like a project or a product                                  |
| `<li>`      | `"li"`      | The card is one of a list, inside a `<ul>` or an `<ol>`                                  |
| `<section>` | `"section"` | The card is a part of the page, like a settings panel, rather than a thing of its own    |
| `<div>`     | `"div"`     | The card is only a box, and its content says everything                                   |

We recommend never making the card itself a link or a button. A card holds a heading, text and sometimes actions of its own, and a link or a button can't hold any of them well: its whole content becomes its name, and it can't have another link or button inside it. When the whole card should take people somewhere, its heading holds the link, as under [Make the heading the link](/docs/components/card#make-the-heading-the-link).

#### Give every card a heading

The heading is how people find a card. Most screen reader users look for what they need on a page by moving from heading to heading, according to every [WebAIM Screen Reader User Survey](https://webaim.org/projects/screenreadersurvey10/), and the heading is what they hear first when they land on a card. It's also what sighted people read first, and what tests and agents look for.

- Every card has a heading that says what it's about, like the project's name ([WCAG 1.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/info-and-relationships.html), [2.4.6](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/headings-and-labels.html)). The Card renders it as an `<h2>` to `<h6>`, so it's a heading for assistive technologies too, rather than bold text.
- Pick the level that fits the page's outline, with `headingLevel`. Cards in a section titled with an `<h2>` get an `<h3>`, which is the default, while cards right under the page's `<h1>` get an `<h2>`. A level that skips one, like an `<h4>` under an `<h2>`, makes people wonder what they missed.
- Give each card in a list a different heading, so people can tell them apart when they list the page's headings or links.

#### Make the heading the link

When the whole card takes people somewhere, like a project's page, put the link in the heading, around the heading's text, and stretch it over the card. `href` does that for you. The link is still just the heading's text, so it's named by the heading alone, while a pseudo-element of the link covers the whole card, so pressing anywhere on the card follows it.

::framework-switcher

- The link's name is the heading, like "Analytical Engine", so screen readers announce a short name that says where it goes, and voice control users can say "click Analytical Engine" ([WCAG 2.4.4](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-in-context.html), [2.5.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/label-in-name.html)). Wrapping the whole card in an `<a>` would make all of its text the link's name, read out every time someone tabs to it.
- It's a real `<a href>`, so it works like every other link. People can open it in a new tab with `Ctrl` or `Cmd`, or with the middle mouse button, copy its address, and see where it goes before they press it. A card with a click handler or `role="link"` loses all of that, and keyboard users can't reach it at all unless it has a `tabindex`.
- Actions, like Save, stay outside the link, in `actions`, and sit above the stretched link, so pressing one runs it rather than following the link. `Tab` reaches them right after the link. HTML doesn't allow a button inside a link, which is one more reason the card itself is never the link.
- A link inside the body or the eyebrow, like a link to the project's author, sits above the stretched link too, so it goes where it says. Keep them few, though, since a card with many links in it is harder to press in the right place.

#### Describe the media

An image that only illustrates the heading, like a photo of the product it names, gets an empty `alt=""`, which tells screen readers to skip it. If its alt text repeated the heading, people would hear the same thing twice. An image that shows something the text doesn't, like a chart or a floor plan, needs alt text that says what it shows ([WCAG 1.1.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-content.html)).

#### Contrast and size

- The text needs a contrast of at least 4.5:1 with the card's background, and so do the eyebrow and the footer, since people need to read them too ([WCAG 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)). Text over an image is hard to keep at 4.5:1, which is why the Card puts its text below the media rather than on top of it.
- The border doesn't need 3:1, since it doesn't tell people anything the heading doesn't ([WCAG 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). The focus ring does, against the page.
- A card that's a link is a large target already, since the whole card follows the link. Its actions are Buttons, which are at least 24 × 24px ([WCAG 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)), with a hit area of 44 × 44px on touch screens. Leave a gap between them, as the actions do, so a finger doesn't press the one next to it.

#### Show focus

When a card's link has focus, the whole card shows an outline in the same focus colour as every other component ([WCAG 2.4.7](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-visible.html)). It's drawn by the link's pseudo-element, which covers the card, so it goes around the whole card rather than the heading's text, which is where people can press. Like the Button's, it's at least 2px thick and drawn outside the border, and we recommend 2px for both. An action shows its own focus ring instead, so only one thing on the page ever looks focused.

You'll also want to keep focused cards clear of sticky headers and footers, which you can do with `scroll-padding` ([WCAG 2.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-minimum.html), [2.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-enhanced.html)).

### Predictable

#### Keyboard

| Key                | Card                                                                        |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `Tab`, `Shift+Tab` | Moves focus to the card's link, if it has one, and then on to its actions   |
| `Enter`            | Follows the link                                                            |

A card has no keys of its own. It's a link and some Buttons, which `Tab` reaches in the order they come in, like on the rest of the page. We recommend not adding arrow keys to move between cards, since people don't expect them on a list of links, and they'd have to find out that they work. A list where the arrow keys do move between items, like a feed that loads more as people scroll, is a pattern of its own, which the APG calls a [Feed](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/feed/).

#### Put the heading first

The heading comes first in the card's DOM, before the eyebrow and the media, while CSS shows the media and the eyebrow above it. People who move from heading to heading land at the start of the card, and hear what it's about before anything else. With an eyebrow or an image before the heading in the DOM, they'd land past them, and miss them.

Moving content around with CSS is only safe for content people can't focus. `Tab` follows the DOM, so if the media held a link or a button, focus would go to the heading's link first and then jump back up to the media. Keep the media an image, and put anything people can press in the body, the actions or the footer.

#### Write headings that say where they go

When a card is a link, its heading is the link's name, so it's best to write it like one: the name of the thing the card is about, rather than "Read more" or "Learn more". People who list the links on a page see the names alone, and five links called "Read more" don't say where any of them go ([WCAG 2.4.4](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-in-context.html)). For the same reason, we recommend not adding a separate "Read more" link next to the stretched one, which would only be a second way to the same place.

#### Don't nest interactive elements

The link holds the heading's text and nothing else. HTML doesn't allow a link or a button inside a link, so nothing defines what browsers and screen readers should do with one. Actions go in `actions`, which is outside the link, and the stretched link only covers them on screen, where the Card lifts them above it.

### Every state

#### Linked

A card that's a link shows that it is when people point at it or tab to it. On hover, its borders get stronger and its heading is underlined, which tells people the whole card does something before they press it. On focus, it shows its focus ring. The pointer over the card is the link's own, so people see the hand they'd see over any other link.

#### With actions

A card can have actions of its own, like Save or Share, whether it's a link or not. They're Buttons, so they keep their names, their states and their focus rings, and pressing one runs it, even on a card that's a link. Once an action has done something, like saving the project, its state shows on the Button, like `aria-pressed` on a Save toggle, rather than on the card.

#### Without media

Not every card has an image, and an image can fail to load. The media keeps its space whatever its image does, with `aspect-ratio`, so cards in a row stay lined up, and the page doesn't jump when an image arrives late. While it loads, or if it fails, the space shows a faint tint of the text colour instead of a gap. If some cards in a list have no image at all, leave the media out of them, rather than showing an empty box.

#### Loading

While cards load, hold their place with placeholders the same size as the cards, so the page doesn't jump around when they arrive. A Skeleton, on our [Roadmap](/docs/getting-started/roadmap), will do that for you. If the cards load because of something people did, like a search, say how many arrived in a status message, since a screen reader user won't see them appear.

#### Long content

Headings and text wrap rather than being cut off, and the card grows to fit them. We recommend not cutting headings off with an ellipsis or a line clamp. People can't read the rest, a screen reader still reads all of it, so the two disagree, and a translated heading is often longer than the original.

### Adaptive

#### Pointer, touch and pen

- Hover styles only apply to pointers that can actually hover (`@media (hover: hover)`), otherwise a tap would leave the card looking hovered.
- On touch screens, the whole card follows its link, so it's an easy target, while its actions are Buttons with hit areas of their own, at least 44 × 44px.
- The stretched link covers the card's text, so pressing and dragging on it drags the link rather than selecting the text. If people need to copy something from a card, like a code or an address, put it on the page the card links to, or leave that card without a link.

#### Motion

The Card doesn't move. If you'd like a card to lift or grow on hover, wrap the animation in `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)`, so it only runs for people who haven't asked for less motion ([WCAG 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)). Keep in mind that a card that grows can cover the ones next to it.

#### More contrast

When someone asks for more contrast (`prefers-contrast: more`), as with Increase contrast on macOS and iOS, the borders are drawn in the text colour at full strength, rather than at 20%. The text, the eyebrow and the footer already pass 4.5:1, so they stay as they are.

#### Forced colours

Windows contrast themes replace every colour with the user's own, and drop backgrounds and shadows. The Card's borders stay, the ones between its sections too, since the system draws them in a colour of its own, focus is drawn with `outline`, in `Highlight`, and the media's `<img>` keeps its image. That's why the Card draws its boundary with a border rather than a shadow, which these themes would drop.

#### Colour scheme

The tokens use `light-dark()`, which follows `color-scheme`, so the Card adapts to your light and dark themes on its own. If you give it a background, use a token that does the same, and check the text against it in both.

#### Text size and spacing

Nothing in the Card has a fixed height, so it grows with its text instead of clipping it, at 200% text size ([WCAG 1.4.4](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/resize-text.html)) and with wider line, letter and word spacing ([WCAG 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)). Long words, like a web address, wrap rather than overflow the card. Only the media keeps its shape, since it's sized by its width and its aspect ratio.

### Consistent

Once you've learned the [Button](/docs/components/button#consistent)'s and the [Input](/docs/components/input#consistent)'s APIs, you already know the Card's `href` and `actions`. These names mean the same thing everywhere in Open Components:

| Name      | Kind | What it means                                                         |
| --------- | ---- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `href`    | Prop | The component is a link, and this is where it goes                    |
| `default` | Slot | The main content, between the component's tags                        |
| `actions` | Slot | Buttons that act on the component, after its main content             |

The rest of the Card's names are its own, and each one says what it is:

- `heading` is the heading, and `headingLevel` its level. We didn't call it `title`, which is an HTML attribute that shows a tooltip, so in Angular, Astro or plain HTML, where an attribute stays on the element, every card would grow one. `label` is for controls, which a card isn't.
- `as` picks the element, as it does in many component libraries. We recommend `article` as the default, since it's what most cards are.
- `media`, `eyebrow` and `footer` are named after the parts they fill, as `actions` is, so the slot and the `data-slot` it renders have the same name.

The Card also leaves a few props out, on purpose:

- There's no `variant` or `color`. A card has no intent of its own, unlike a Button, and its look comes from its variables, which you set from a class.
- There's no `disabled`. A card isn't a control, so there's nothing to disable. If its link isn't available, like a project that's been archived, leave out the `href` and say why in the body.
- There's no `selected`. A card people pick, like a plan, is a form control that looks like a card, which a Radio Group or a Checkbox on our [Roadmap](/docs/getting-started/roadmap) will cover, with the keyboard and the states that a choice needs.
- There's no `onClick`. The Card never listens for clicks, since its link does that job, and its actions are Buttons with listeners of their own.

### Type-safe

Props and slots are typed, so your editor can autocomplete them and your type checker (`tsc`, `vue-tsc`, `svelte-check`, `ngc` or `astro check`) catches mistakes like `headingLevel={1}`, which would give a card the page's own heading level.

| Prop           | Type                                       | Default     | Description                                                              |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `as`           | `"article" \| "section" \| "div" \| "li"`  | `"article"` | The element it renders                                                   |
| `heading`      | `string`                                   |             | The heading, when it's plain text. The `heading` slot takes precedence    |
| `headingLevel` | `2 \| 3 \| 4 \| 5 \| 6`                    | `3`         | The heading's level, which fits the page's outline                       |
| `href`         | `string`                                   |             | Links the card through its heading, stretched over the whole card        |

The Card doesn't fire events of its own. Its link and its actions fire theirs, like `click`, which you listen to on them.

| Slot      | Description                                                                                  |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `default` | The body, like a sentence or two and some details                                            |
| `heading` | The heading, when it's more than plain text, or your router's link, which is stretched too   |
| `eyebrow` | A short line shown above the heading, like a category, which comes after it in the DOM       |
| `media`   | An image, shown at the top, which comes after the heading in the DOM                         |
| `actions` | Buttons that act on the card, like Save, which sit above the stretched link                  |
| `footer`  | A last section, set apart by a border, for details about the card, like who made it or when  |

### Composable

The root element is the card itself, the `<article>` or whichever element `as` picks, so everything you pass lands directly on it. That includes classes, styles, `id`, `dir`, `lang`, ARIA attributes and event listeners. A ref to the Card reaches that same element, so there's no wrapper to reach through.

If you'd like an `<article>` to have a name of its own, give the heading's text an `id`, through the `heading` slot, and point the card's `aria-labelledby` at it. Keep in mind that a `<section>` with a name becomes a region landmark, which people can jump to, so only name a `section` card when it's one of the main parts of the page.

#### Links and routers

`href` renders a plain `<a>`. For links that go through your router, put your router's link in the `heading` slot instead, and the Card stretches it over the card just the same, since it stretches any link in its heading. Routers that handle every link on the page only need the `href`:

::framework-switcher
#react
```tsx
import { Link } from "react-router";

<Card heading={<Link to="/projects/analytical-engine">Analytical Engine</Link>}>
  <p>A general-purpose mechanical computer.</p>
</Card>
```

```vue
<Card>
  <template #heading>
    <RouterLink to="/projects/analytical-engine">Analytical Engine</RouterLink>
  </template>
  <p>A general-purpose mechanical computer.</p>
</Card>
```

Since the Card doesn't know anything about your router, it works with any of them.

#### With other components

- Actions are [Buttons](/docs/components/button), which keep their names, states and focus rings, and sit above the stretched link. We recommend `sm` Buttons, ranked `outline` or `ghost`, so a page of cards doesn't fill up with primary actions.
- A [Spinner](/docs/components/spinner) doesn't belong in a card while it loads. A Skeleton, on our [Roadmap](/docs/getting-started/roadmap), will hold the card's shape instead.
- A Badge, also on our Roadmap, fits in the eyebrow, to mark a status like "New".
- A card that opens a Dialog rather than a page gets a Button for it in `actions`, rather than a stretched link, since a link should always go somewhere.

### Controllable

The Card doesn't keep any state of its own. Everything about it is a prop, an attribute or an element that you control:

| What          | How you control it                                                   |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Element       | `as`                                                                 |
| Heading level | `headingLevel`                                                       |
| Link          | `href`, or a link in the `heading` slot                              |
| Hover, focus  | The browser, from the link                                           |
| Focus         | The link's own `focus()`, through a ref to the card or to your link  |

## Agentic Experience (AX)

Finally, let's look at how agents read, build and check a card. They rely on the same accessibility tree as assistive technologies, and on a contract they can check the code against.

### Semantic

Agents that drive a browser, through Playwright or a browser MCP server, read the page through its accessibility tree, which is made of roles, names and states. This is how they see the cards under [Layout](/docs/components/card#layout):

```yaml
- list:
  - listitem:
    - heading "Analytical Engine" [level=3]:
      - link "Analytical Engine":
        - /url: /projects/analytical-engine
    - paragraph: A general-purpose mechanical computer, first described in 1837.
    - button "Save"
  - listitem:
    - heading "Difference Engine" [level=3]:
      - link "Difference Engine":
        - /url: /projects/difference-engine
    - paragraph: A machine for working out tables of numbers.
    - button "Save"
```

The images are left out, since their `alt` is empty. A card that's a `<div>` with a click handler shows up as a heading and some text, with nothing to say that pressing it does anything, so an agent can't follow it any more than a keyboard user can. With a real link, named by the heading, an agent can find the card and follow it, or find one of its actions:

```ts
await page.getByRole("link", { name: "Analytical Engine" }).click();

const card = page.getByRole("listitem").filter({ has: page.getByRole("heading", { name: "Analytical Engine" }) });
await card.getByRole("button", { name: "Save" }).click();
```

### Described

Here's the whole contract in one block, for agents to read before they build or review a card:

```yaml
component: Card
summary: Groups what people need to know about one thing, like a project or a product, under a heading. With an href, its heading is a link stretched over the whole card.
standard: https://opencomponents.dev/docs/components/card
element: article # or section, div or li, with as
role: article # listitem as an li. A section is a region only once it has a name
props:
  as: { type: [article, section, div, li], default: article }
  heading: { type: string } # the heading slot takes precedence
  headingLevel: { type: [2, 3, 4, 5, 6], default: 3 }
  href: { type: string } # links the card through its heading
slots:
  default: The body, like a sentence or two and some details.
  heading: The heading, when it's more than plain text, or a router's link, which is stretched too.
  eyebrow: A short line shown above the heading, like a category, which comes after it in the DOM.
  media: An image, shown at the top, which comes after the heading in the DOM.
  actions: Buttons that act on the card, like Save, which sit above the stretched link.
  footer: A last section, set apart by a border, for details about the card, like who made it or when.
states:
  hover: ":has(> [data-slot=header] > [data-slot=heading] a[href]:hover), in @media (hover: hover)"
  focus: "[data-slot=heading] a[href]:focus-visible, drawn around the card by its ::after"
keyboard:
  Tab: Moves focus to the card's link, then to its actions.
  Enter: Follows the link.
parts: [header, heading, link, eyebrow, media, body, actions, footer] # data-slot, in this order. The link is in the heading
tokens:
  theme: ["--color--neutral-text", "--color--focus"]
  variables: [--card--padding, --card--radius, --card--border-color, --card--background, --card--media--aspect-ratio]
  layer: components # in Tailwind's order: theme, base, components, utilities
rules: https://opencomponents.dev/docs/components/card#checklist
```

It's also published on its own at [`/raw/docs/components/card.yaml`](/raw/docs/components/card.yaml){external=""}, with every rule from the checklist in place of the `rules` link. It's a fraction of this page's length, so it's the one to give agents that only need the rules, and not the reasoning behind them.

Every rule has a stable ID, like `card/stretched-link`, so you and your agents can cite it in reviews and commits.

### Deterministic

The same props always render the same markup, leaving out anything your framework adds for itself, like comments or scoping attributes. For example, here's a card in a list, that links to a project:

```html
<Card as="li" heading="Analytical Engine" href="/projects/analytical-engine">
  <p>A general-purpose mechanical computer.</p>
</Card>
```

And here's what it renders:

```html
<li class="card">
  <div data-slot="header">
    <h3 data-slot="heading">
      <a data-slot="link" href="/projects/analytical-engine">Analytical Engine</a>
    </h3>
  </div>
  <div data-slot="body">
    <p>A general-purpose mechanical computer.</p>
  </div>
</li>
```

- The element is always the one `as` names, and the heading's level always the one `headingLevel` names, so neither is guessed from where the card is on the page.
- Parts are marked with `data-slot` and always come in the same order: the `header`, which holds the `heading`, its `link` and the `eyebrow`, then the `media`, the `body`, the `actions` and the `footer`. Only the header and the heading are always there, and the others render when they're given.
- Hover and focus are styles, never changes to the markup, so focus and the screen reader's position survive both.
- There's nothing random or generated, like IDs or timestamps, so the markup is the same on the server, on the client and in every snapshot.

### Verifiable

Every rule in the checklist can be checked, and about a third of them automatically.

#### Unit tests

Every rule that the checklist checks with a unit test has one in `Card.test.ts`, which you'll find under [Reference implementation](/docs/components/card#reference-implementation). We run them on every change to this page, so the code you see always passes them.

#### In the browser

Unit tests run without a layout, so they can't press the card's text and check that the stretched link catches it. In the browser, you can press the card the way people would, check that its actions don't follow the link, and run [axe](https://github.com/dequelabs/axe-core) to check contrast, alt text and the list:

```ts
import AxeBuilder from "@axe-core/playwright";
import { expect, test } from "@playwright/test";

test("a project card saves from its actions, and follows its link from anywhere else", async ({ page }) => {
  await page.goto("/projects");

  const card = page.getByRole("listitem").filter({ has: page.getByRole("heading", { name: "Analytical Engine" }) });
  await card.getByRole("button", { name: "Save" }).click();
  await expect(page).toHaveURL(/\/projects$/);

  const { violations } = await new AxeBuilder({ page })
    .withTags(["wcag2a", "wcag2aa", "wcag21aa", "wcag22aa"])
    .analyze();
  expect(violations).toEqual([]);

  // The middle of the card is its body text, which the stretched link covers.
  await card.click();
  await expect(page).toHaveURL(/\/projects\/analytical-engine$/);
});
```

Some things still need a human, so try your cards with the keyboard alone, with a screen reader, where you can list the page's headings and links, on a phone, and at 200% zoom, with forced colours and more contrast turned on. You can emulate the last two in Chrome DevTools, under Rendering.

## Checklist

Here's every rule in one place, along with where it comes from and how to check it. A card meets the standard when it meets every **must**, and each **should** is expected unless you have a good reason not to follow it.

The scope says who meets a rule. **Component** rules are met by the Card itself, so they're the ones to check when you build or review one. **Usage** rules are met by the pages that use it, like describing its images, so they're the ones to check when you review a screen. **Both** rules need the two to work together, like its heading: the Card renders one at the level you give it, as long as you give it a heading that says what the card is about.

The basis says where a rule comes from. About half the rules rest on **WCAG** 2.2, which we follow at level AA, and name the success criteria behind them, and one comes from **HTML**. The rest are **Open Components** rules, which are our own, like putting the heading first in the DOM. Where a rule goes further than WCAG asks at level AA, its basis says so: some come from level **AAA**, like keeping cards still for people who ask for less motion, and a rule that asks for more than its criterion says **beyond**, like the stretched link, where WCAG would let a card be a link in other ways.

## Reference implementation

Our Vue 3 reference implementation does its part of every rule on this page, and powers every example on it. Feel free to copy it into your design system and change how it looks, but try to keep how it behaves. It reads the same theme tokens as the Button, so copy [`tokens.css`](/docs/components/button#reference-implementation) from the Button's reference implementation into your theme too, and its examples put [Buttons](/docs/components/button#reference-implementation) in the footer.

The tests are in `Card.test.ts`. To run them, you'll need [Vitest](https://vitest.dev), set to `environment: "jsdom"` and `globals: true`, along with `@testing-library/vue`, `@testing-library/user-event` and `@testing-library/jest-dom`.

::code-collapse
:::code-group
```vue [Card.vue]
<script setup lang="ts">
export interface CardProps {
  /** The element it renders: `li` in a list of cards, or `section` or `div` for a card that isn't self-contained. */
  as?: "article" | "section" | "div" | "li";
  /** The heading, when it's plain text. The `heading` slot takes precedence. It isn't `title`, which is an HTML attribute that shows a tooltip. */
  heading?: string;
  /** The heading's level, which fits the page's outline: 3 under a section's `<h2>`. */
  headingLevel?: 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6;
  /** Links the card through its heading, and stretches the link over the whole card. */
  href?: string;
}

const { as = "article", heading, headingLevel = 3, href } = defineProps<CardProps>();

defineSlots<{
  /** The body, like a sentence or two and some details. */
  default?: () => unknown;
  /** The heading, when it's more than plain text, or your router's link. A link here stretches over the card too. */
  heading?: () => unknown;
  /** A short line shown above the heading, like a category, which comes after it in the DOM. */
  eyebrow?: () => unknown;
  /** An image, shown at the top, which comes after the heading in the DOM. */
  media?: () => unknown;
  /** Buttons that act on the card, like Save, which sit above the stretched link and stay reachable with Tab. */
  actions?: () => unknown;
  /** A last section, set apart by a border, for details about the card, like who made it or when. */
  footer?: () => unknown;
}>();
</script>

<template>
  <!-- The heading comes first in the DOM, so heading navigation lands at the start of
       the card. The media and the eyebrow come after it, and CSS shows them above it. -->
  <component :is="as" class="card">
    <div data-slot="header">
      <component :is="`h${headingLevel}`" data-slot="heading">
        <a v-if="href" data-slot="link" :href="href">
          <slot name="heading">{{ heading }}</slot>
        </a>
        <slot v-else name="heading">{{ heading }}</slot>
      </component>
      <p v-if="$slots.eyebrow" data-slot="eyebrow">
        <slot name="eyebrow" />
      </p>
    </div>
    <div v-if="$slots.media" data-slot="media">
      <slot name="media" />
    </div>
    <div v-if="$slots.default" data-slot="body">
      <slot />
    </div>
    <div v-if="$slots.actions" data-slot="actions">
      <slot name="actions" />
    </div>
    <div v-if="$slots.footer" data-slot="footer">
      <slot name="footer" />
    </div>
  </component>
</template>

<style scoped>
/* The same layer order as Tailwind CSS. The card's styles go in `components`,
   so they win over the resets in `base`, and give way to utilities and to any
   style outside a layer, like a class of yours that sets its variables. */
@layer theme, base, components, utilities;

@layer components {
  .card {
    --card--padding: 1.25rem;
    --card--radius: 0.75rem;
    /* The card's border, and the ones that set its header and footer apart, are decoration:
       its heading says what it is, so they don't need 3:1. */
    --card--border-color: color-mix(in srgb, currentColor 20%, transparent);
    --card--background: transparent;
    --card--media--aspect-ratio: 16 / 9;

    /* The containing block of the stretched link. */
    position: relative;
```

## FAQ

#### Card or something else?

A card summarises one thing, so people can scan a few of them and pick one. For anything else, another component will do a better job:

| When people need to…                                           | Use                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Scan a few things and pick one, like projects or products      | Cards, in a list                                            |
| Compare many things by their details, like prices and dates    | Table                                                       |
| Pick one option, like a plan, as part of a form                | Radio Group, which can look like a set of cards             |
| Be told something important, like an error                     | Alert                                                       |
| Read through a stream that loads more as they scroll           | A list of cards, built on the APG's [Feed](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/feed/) pattern |

The Table, the Radio Group and the Alert are on our [Roadmap](/docs/getting-started/roadmap).

::accordion
:::accordion-item{label="Why not wrap the whole card in a link?"}
Because a link's name is all of its text. A screen reader would read the whole card every time someone tabs to it, and voice control users would have to say all of it to press it. A link can't hold a button either, so a card wrapped in one can't have actions of its own. A link in the heading, stretched over the card, is pressed the same way, and named by the heading alone.
:::

:::accordion-item{label="Can people select the text in a card that's a link?"}
Not by dragging across it, since the stretched link covers it, and dragging a link drags its address. That's the trade-off for a whole card people can press. Keep text people might copy, like a code or an address, on the page the card links to, or leave that card without a link. Making the card a link with a click handler wouldn't help either, since it would trade the selection for everything a real link does.
:::

:::accordion-item{label="Should a card's link open in a new tab?"}
No more than any other link. Links that open new tabs on their own take people away from the page they were on, and break the back button. People who want a new tab can open one with `Ctrl` or `Cmd`, or the middle mouse button, which is one of the things a real link gives them.
:::

:::accordion-item{label="Can a card have a shadow?"}
Of course, from a class, if that suits your design. Keep the border too, though, even a faint one, since forced colours modes drop shadows, and a card drawn with a shadow alone would lose its edge in them.
:::

:::accordion-item{label="Does the Card work before JavaScript loads?"}
Yes, completely. Everything it does is HTML and CSS, so the server renders a real `<article>`, a real heading and a real link, which work before the page hydrates, and even without JavaScript at all. Only your actions, like Save, wait for your script.
:::
